How to use this guide

The novella has five unnumbered chapters. References take the form 3 (40%): chapter 3, about 40 per cent of the way through, rounded to the nearest 5 per cent. This works in any edition of Gregory Rabassa’s translation.

Built for Paper 2. The issue arcs trace each concern through the five chapters, and Dig deeper sets out readings to test against the novella.

The examination

Course and paperIB Diploma English A: Language and Literature, Paper 2: the comparative essay on two literary works studied in the course. Paper 2 is not a set-text exam: the questions are general, they do not name any work, and there are no extracts.
Format1 hour 45 minutes at both SL and HL. Answer one of four general questions, comparing two of the literary works you have studied. 25 marks.
CriteriaA Knowledge, understanding and interpretation (5). B Analysis and evaluation (10), in two strands: B1 how meaning is shaped by language, technique, style and broader authorial choices (5); B2 the similarities and differences between the two works (5). C Focus and organisation (5). D Language (5).
What this means for revisionYou will not know the question in advance, so you cannot prepare a single essay. You prepare a small number of moments from each work that you know in detail, grouped by issue, and you rehearse how each work handles those issues differently. That is what the issue arcs, the event bank and the quote bank in this guide are for. Comparison must be structural: every paragraph needs both works, and criterion B2 assesses it directly.
iSWIM for Paper 2. Use the five guiding questions on every prompt.
iWhat is the issue of significance? The concern beneath the prompt’s topic word.
SWhy does it matter? The human, social or philosophical stakes.
WWhat does each author want you to understand about it?
IHow does each author get you to understand it? Structure, form, characterisation, voice, staging.
MWhat does each work position its audience towards, and what does the comparison reveal? The essay-level M comes from the tension between M(A) and M(B).

Write each paragraph with PEACE ACT: Point, Evidence, Analysis, Comparison, Extension, Audience, Context, Tie-back. Characters do not exist: reach through the characters to the choices the writer makes with them.

iSWIM tells you how to plan. Thinking with iSWIM tells you how to think. Your text-type guide tells you what to notice and what to call it.

Context

Facts about the writer and period are attributed to a named source. Facts about the work come from the work.

The real case

The novella is based on the murder of Cayetano Gentile Chimento in Sucre, Colombia, in 1951. Gentile was a friend of García Márquez’s family, and García Márquez waited many years before writing about it.

Source: LitCharts

Author and form

García Márquez worked for many years as a journalist; the novella was published in 1981, the year before he won the Nobel Prize. It is often read as a detective story and as part of the Latin American Boom.

Source: LitCharts

Event bank

16 numbered events in order. Read one chapter for close revision, or E1 to E16 straight through as the arc of the whole work.

Chapter 1

E1 The opening sentence 1 (0%)

The first sentence announces the murder and the hour Santiago Nasar got up 1 (0%); the narrator gathers memories twenty-seven years later 1 (0%).

Why it matters. The ending is given away at once: suspense moves from what happens to how it could have been allowed to happen.

Dig deeper. If we know the ending, what keeps us reading?

E2 Waiting for the bishop 1 (25%)

The town gathers to greet the bishop’s boat; Santiago’s mother predicts the bishop will not land 1 (25%).

Why it matters. The church passes by on the river while a death is prepared in the square.

E3 The kitchen 1 (25–30%)

Victoria Guzmán and her daughter Divina Flor serve Santiago coffee 1 (30%); Victoria had been seduced by his father 1 (30%).

Why it matters. Santiago’s household contains its own history of sexual power, set beside the accusation against him.

E4 The twins wait 1 (55%)

The Vicario twins wait for Santiago in Clotilde Armenta’s milk shop opposite his house 1 (55%).

Why it matters. The killers wait in public view.

E5 The returned bride 1 (90%)

The narrator’s sister learns that Angela Vicario, married the day before, has been returned to her parents because she was not a virgin 1 (90%).

Why it matters. The motive is revealed only after the killing has been fixed as certain.

Chapter 2

E6 Bayardo San Román arrives 2 (0–5%)

Bayardo San Román, a rich outsider, arrives and decides to marry Angela 2 (0%); he wins her family with money, buying every raffle ticket 2 (20%).

Why it matters. Marriage is a transaction; Angela’s mother arranges it over Angela’s doubts.

E7 The widower Xius’s house 2 (45%)

Bayardo buys the widower Xius’s house for Angela, overriding the old man’s reluctance 2 (45%).

Why it matters. Money buys even memories that are not for sale.

E8 The wedding night and the name 2 (55–100%)

Angela’s friends tell her how to fake virginity 2 (55%); she refuses to deceive. Returned home and beaten, she names Santiago Nasar 2 (100%).

Why it matters. The name is given in a single sentence with no evidence.

Dig deeper. The narrator never confirms that Santiago was guilty. What does the novella gain by leaving it open?

Chapter 3

E9 Honour and the surrender 3 (0–5%)

The court accepts that the killing defended honour 3 (0%). The twins surrender to Father Amador: they killed openly but are innocent 3 (0%).

Why it matters. The law confirms the code that made the murder possible.

E10 Telling everyone 3 (10–15%)

The twins sharpen their knives at the meat market and tell everyone what they mean to do 3 (10%); their reputation as good people means no one believes them 3 (15%).

Why it matters. The twins seem to want to be stopped.

Dig deeper. Why do the twins announce the murder to everyone they meet?

E11 The mayor 3 (30%)

Colonel Lázaro Aponte takes the twins’ knives 3 (30%) and considers the matter closed; they fetch others.

Why it matters. Authority acts once and then goes to play dominoes.

Chapter 4

E12 The autopsy 4 (0–10%)

Father Amador, in the doctor’s absence, performs a brutal autopsy 4 (0%).

Why it matters. The body is violated a second time, by a priest.

E13 Angela’s letters 4 (90–100%)

Angela writes to Bayardo for years 4 (90%); he returns with almost two thousand letters 4 (100%), all unopened 4 (100%).

Why it matters. The returned bride becomes the author of her own love story.

Dig deeper. Is Angela the novella’s most independent character, or its most obsessed?

Chapter 5

E14 The judge’s notes 5 (5–70%)

The investigating magistrate, reading Nietzsche, fills the brief with marginal notes 5 (10%), including that fatality makes us invisible 5 (70%).

Why it matters. The law turns to philosophy because it cannot explain how a town let it happen.

E15 The note under the door 5 (35%)

A warning note had been slipped under Santiago’s door; no one saw it 5 (35%).

Why it matters. One of many chances that fail.

E16 The killing 5 (80–100%)

His mother, believing he is upstairs, slams and bars the front door 5 (85%), and Santiago is killed against it. He walks round to the back, holding his entrails 5 (100%), and falls in the kitchen 5 (100%).

Why it matters. The novella ends at the moment of death, not after it.

García Márquez’s methods

AO3: how the writer creates meanings and effects through language, structure and form.

Inverted suspense

The end is given in the first sentence 1 (0%); the novella builds suspense around how, not whether.

The chronicle and the journalist narrator

The narrator writes like a reporter, quoting witnesses and dates 1 (0%).

Non-linear structure

Each chapter returns to the morning of the murder from a different angle and ends with the death foreshadowed or achieved 5 (100%).

Dig deeper

The surface overview is in the event bank and issue arcs. These sections go further: the real case behind the novella, the chronicle form, honour, fate and responsibility, and religion and sacrifice. Every claim about the novella is referenced; critical readings are offered for evaluation.

Foreknowledge and responsibility

On the surface. The death was fated.

It could be argued that the novella’s real subject is not fate but collective inaction: the twins announce their plan 3 (10%), the mayor disarms them once, a warning note lies unseen 5 (35%), and the magistrate falls back on fatality 5 (70%).

Questions to explore
  • How many people could have stopped the murder? Why did none of them?

Honour as performance

On the surface. The twins kill for honour.

The court accepts honour as a defence 3 (0%), and the narrator’s mother says honour is love 5 (5%). One reading is that the twins want to be stopped, and kill because no one stops them: honour is something performed for the town.

Questions to explore
  • Is honour a private value or a public performance in the novella?

The chronicle form

On the surface. A detective story.

The narrator returns twenty-seven years later 1 (0%) and assembles testimony like a journalist, but never reaches a verdict on Santiago’s guilt. It could be read as a detective story with the solution withheld, a form that critics have compared with Borges and Kafka.

Questions to explore
  • Why does the narrator never decide whether Santiago was guilty?

Religion and sacrifice

On the surface. A murder.

The bishop will not land 1 (25%), a priest performs the autopsy 4 (0%), and Santiago dies in a ritual manner in public. It could be argued that the novella presents the killing as a sacrifice that the community requires.

Questions to explore
  • What role does the church play in the death?
